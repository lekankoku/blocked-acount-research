The Story

Why this market exists — and why it's broken

Every year, hundreds of thousands of non-EU nationals who want to study or work in Germany hit the same wall. German immigration law (§2 Abs. 3 Aufenthaltsgesetz) says a visa is only granted if you can prove you can support yourself. The standard way to prove it is a blocked account — a Sperrkonto — where you park a full year of living costs (€11,904 for students in 2026) that the provider then drip-feeds back to you in monthly instalments once you arrive. It is effectively mandatory, and two companies — Expatrio and Fintiba — dominate it.

What is a blocked account?

A special escrow account required for a German student or job-seeker visa. You deposit ~12 months of living expenses up front. The money is legally frozen and only released to you in fixed monthly amounts after you enter Germany, register your address, and verify your identity. It proves to the embassy that you won't become destitute.

Why is it a business?

Providers charge a setup fee (€49–159) plus a monthly service fee (€5–10), bundle health insurance, and hold large deposits. Demand is non-discretionary and tied to visa deadlines, so customers must use someone. That makes it a reliable, high-volume market — but one where trust and speed matter enormously, because a delay can cost someone their visa.

The one-paragraph thesis

If you only read one box

This is a large, deadline-driven market that customers are forced into, served by two incumbents whose back-offices buckle under seasonal load. The money-in experience is slick; everything after — verification, funding reconciliation, monthly payouts, refunds, and human support — is fragile. A new entrant that treats reliability and human support as the product (not a cost centre), starts in India/Turkey/China, and anchors on the postgraduate STEM student, can carve out ~€80M of annual deposits in three years even against entrenched players. The rest of this dashboard is the evidence.

Glossary — the German terms you'll see everywhere

Sperrkonto
The blocked account itself — the frozen deposit that proves financial means.
Sperrbestätigung (Doc 06)
The official blocking confirmation the embassy needs to grant the visa. Delays here miss visa appointments.
Sperrfreigabe
An official release letter from the authorities. Providers demand it before refunding — even when no visa was issued, causing deadlocks.
Anmeldung
Mandatory city registration of your address after arrival. Needed to open a local bank account — which is needed to receive payouts.
PostIdent / IDnow / WebID
Identity-verification methods — in person at Deutsche Post (PostIdent) or by video call (IDnow/WebID).
Ausländerbehörde
The local immigration office that issues residence permits and sets document deadlines.
Chancenkarte (§20a)
The Opportunity Card — a points-based visa for skilled job-seekers. Higher deposit (€13,092).
Aufenthaltstitel / Fiktionsbescheinigung
Residence permit / a temporary certificate bridging the wait for the physical permit.
Verpflichtungserklärung (§68)
A sponsor's formal declaration of commitment — an alternative to a blocked account, so these people never become customers.
M10
The electronic proof-of-health-insurance notification that must reach the university, or enrolment is blocked.
BAföG rate
The federal student-aid benchmark that sets the required monthly deposit (€992/mo study, €1,091/mo Opportunity Card).
MKT-01 · MKT-02

How big is the prize?

Sizing a market like this is not one number — it's a funnel. We start from everyone the law could push toward a blocked account, strip out those who prove funds another way or that we can't serve, then estimate what a brand-new entrant can realistically capture against two incumbents. Below, every layer is explained in plain English before the charts.

First, the three layers — in plain English

TAM — the whole pond

Total Addressable Market. Every non-EU visa/residence episode in a year that legally needs proof of funds. We count account opportunities, not people (one person can trigger more than one). The defensible 2025 ceiling is 128,045 episodes ≈ €1.56B if every one funded the full statutory amount.

SAM — who we can serve

Serviceable Addressable Market. TAM minus the people we can't or won't convert: those who prove funds another way, sanctioned nationalities, and drop-off at verification/transfer. Base case: 94,714 accounts ≈ €1.15B.

SOM — what we can win

Serviceable Obtainable Market. The share a new brand can realistically capture in years 1–3 given distribution and brand, against entrenched incumbents. Base: 6,579 accounts ≈ €79.6M (range 1,885–14,738).

What "leaks out" between TAM and SAM — and why. Not everyone who could use a blocked account does. Three filters remove them: (1) Alternative proof of funds (12–25%) — scholarships (DAAD), a sponsor's Verpflichtungserklärung, or an employment salary; these people never open one. (2) Sanctions / FATCA (~3%) — nationalities a provider legally cannot serve. (3) KYC & FX friction (4–12%) — people who start but can't get verified or can't move the money across. Then a capture rate (2–15%) turns the serviceable market into what a new brand actually wins.

Why customers are furious — and what we'd build

We read 3,024 public reviews of Expatrio and Fintiba across Trustpilot, Google Play, Google Maps, the App Store and Trustindex. A clear pattern emerges: the software that takes your money is slick, but everything that happens after — verification, funding reconciliation, monthly payouts, refunds and human support — is fragile and breaks under seasonal load. Below, each major pain-point is explained (what's happening, why, a real customer voice) and paired with 💡 the product we'd build to kill it.

Provider comparative analysis — Expatrio vs Fintiba

Both incumbents are support-starved, but they fail in different places. Knowing where each is weakest tells a new entrant exactly which corridors and moments to attack.

Fintiba fails harder on…

Blocking-confirmation delays. Fintiba holds 89.6% of all such complaints (69 vs 8 for Expatrio). Its Sutor→FIB bank migration stalled document generation for weeks — 40-day waits for the Sperrbestätigung are common.

Source-of-funds AML holds & refunds. Refund friction runs 7.74% of Fintiba reviews vs 4.53% for Expatrio, and its FIB compliance freezes CIS/South-Asian corridors for 1–3 months.

Expatrio fails harder on…

Post-arrival payout activation. Expatrio carries 53.7% of payout-activation complaints (188 vs 162) — mostly PostIdent sync loops and the +49-number / IBAN catch-22.

Identity/KYC & forced FX. 56.4% of KYC complaints (212 vs 164), plus the mandatory Flywire corridor that skims €200–300 from Chinese & Indian transfers.

Why it breaks — the four structural weaknesses

Every pain-point above traces back to one of four systemic flaws. A new entrant that is structurally different on these four wins on trust, not features.

The hidden cost of ownership

Headline setup fees hide the real cost. Expatrio: €49–89 setup + €5/mo + €100 buffer. Fintiba: €89–159 setup + €4.90–9.90/mo + €100 buffer. Coracle: €59–99 one-time, €0/mo — the pricing benchmark to beat.

The benchmark: Coracle

Reviews repeatedly cite Coracle as the positive contrast — no monthly fee, and faster, low-bureaucracy refunds after visa denials. Any new entrant must at least match Coracle's refund speed and fee transparency to win on trust.

The four hypotheses we set out to test

H1 · Account-creation delays → PARTLY TRUE. Sign-up itself is fast (<15 min). The delay is downstream, when registration collides with document review or passport screening. So the real problem isn't the form — it's verification.
H2 · Identity-verification failures → CONFIRMED (critical). Video KYC rejects newer passports; PostIdent "success" silently fails to sync, forcing 3–6 repeat post-office trips. A top-3 blocker.
H3 · Slow visa-rejection refunds → CONFIRMED (catastrophic). Lower frequency but highest severity: 2–10 months (sometimes years) to return €11k+, with fees still draining the trapped balance.
H4 · Support bottlenecks → CONFIRMED (ecosystem-wide). The single most-mentioned issue (40%). Deflection bots block human escalation; email takes 3 days–6 weeks. This amplifies every other pain-point.

Beyond the hypotheses — what the data surfaced

The four hypotheses above were what we set out to test. Coding the full corpus surfaced six emergent pain-points nobody briefed us on — two of them (funding reconciliation, app glitches) are among the most frequent in the entire dataset.

IDEmergent pain-pointSeverityFrequencyWhat it is

Deduction: the mandatory hypotheses captured the most severe failures (refunds, KYC, support), but missed the two highest-frequency ones — funding reconciliation (24.75%) and app reliability (37.18%). A feasibility study anchored only on prior assumptions would have under-invested in exactly the problems customers hit most.

The bright spots — proof the "happy path" works

When it works, it's great. With a standard passport, an unrestricted SEPA transfer, and no consular hiccup, accounts are opened and the Sperrbestätigung issued within 24–48 hours. The core product is sound — it's the exceptions that break.
Bundling is valued. Value packages (blocked account + travel + public health insurance) get positive sentiment when the automation fires correctly, saving students €100+ in setup. The opportunity is to make the exception path as good as the happy path.

What we can — and can't — conclude

Public reviews are strong evidence for what breaks and why it hurts, but they have real limits. Naming them keeps the analysis honest and points to what primary research must still verify.

Net confidence: high on direction, medium on magnitude. The ranking of pain-points is robust — it repeats across five independent platforms and two providers. The absolute percentages are upper bounds skewed by negative-reporting bias, so read them as "share of complaints", not "share of customers".

Every top-risk failure happens at a boundary the provider doesn't control

This is the single deduction that ties the whole dataset together. The onboarding software is fine on the happy path; it buckles wherever the provider has to hand off to an external system it can't govern — a correspondent bank, a KYC vendor, a consulate, a city-registration office. Neither Expatrio nor Fintiba is a licensed bank, so most failures below are really a third party's failure passed straight to the customer. Then support amplifies all of it. Every one of these third-party failures lands in a Jul–Sep support queue staffed by deflection bots — so a correspondent-bank skim or a PostIdent sync glitch becomes a three-week ordeal with no human to call. The wedge: a new entrant that owns these four handoffs — absorbing shortfalls, running multi-vendor KYC failover, honouring the rejection letter, and decoupling month-1 payout from the Anmeldung — neutralises the category's highest-risk pain-points in a single stroke.
